## Problem
Solar installations are large, repetitive, and difficult to inspect panel by panel. A thermal camera can turn temperature differences into a visible pattern, but that is only the start of the job. Reflections, hot surroundings, junctions, and image texture can all create bright regions that look important at first glance.

The useful outcome is not simply a colourful thermal frame. It is a short, reviewable list of locations that may need a technician to inspect.

## What I built
Cleanoov brings that review process into a focused thermal hotspot detector. The recorded interface accepts a video file or a live stream, displays the thermal image, draws candidate boxes, and reports the number of current candidates.

The controls expose the decisions that change a detector's behaviour: sensitivity, the largest permitted box as a percentage of the frame, minimum spot size, texture filtering, and a cluster filter. That makes the tool an inspection aid with visible tuning controls rather than a black box that silently decides what is faulty.

The project also includes a solar-cleaning robot render. It is a concept for the operational side of the wider idea, not evidence of a tested autonomous robot.

## How it works
### Start from thermal contrast, then constrain the result
The interface starts by identifying bright thermal regions relative to the rest of the frame. Raising sensitivity restricts the candidates to hotter regions; lowering it can reveal weaker temperature differences but also introduces more noise. A field workflow needs both settings because a threshold that works at one time of day or with one camera palette may fail elsewhere.

### Reject shapes that do not fit the inspection task
A fault candidate should have a plausible size in the image. The maximum-box control can discard very large warm areas, such as a whole roof zone, while the minimum-spot control removes tiny isolated pixels. These are practical safeguards: without them, a detector can spend its time reporting things that are either too broad to localise or too small to investigate.

### Use texture and density as evidence, not as a verdict
Thermal images contain texture from module boundaries, ground, reflections, and compression. The texture filter offers a way to suppress candidates that do not have the local variation expected by the chosen settings. The cluster filter addresses another failure mode: many overlapping boxes can turn one confusing region into a long list of alerts. Grouping or suppressing dense clusters makes the overlay easier to read.

These controls are only a first pass. A box is a candidate for inspection, not a confirmed electrical fault. Confirmation needs context such as panel layout, irradiance, camera calibration, repeat observations, and where appropriate an electrical measurement.

## Result
The supplied evidence shows a working inspection interface with video and stream controls, adjustable filters, and hotspot overlays on a solar-array thermal frame. It demonstrates the review workflow and the kinds of false positives the controls are intended to manage.

It does not establish detection accuracy, camera calibration, fault-classification performance, or robot field performance. Those require labelled thermal data and on-site validation.

## What I would improve
I would add a frame-by-frame review queue that keeps the source timestamp, panel-row position, detector settings, and an operator decision for every alert. Requiring a candidate to persist across adjacent frames would reduce one-frame noise. A calibration and validation plan should compare the alerts with confirmed field findings, then report precision and missed defects separately for different weather conditions.

For the robot concept, the next concrete milestone is a safe manual or supervised platform test: traction, panel-safe contact forces, water use, obstacle handling, and an emergency-stop path all need evidence before autonomy becomes a meaningful claim.
