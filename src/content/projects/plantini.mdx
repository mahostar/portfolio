## Abstract
Plantini combines a modular, fabricated hydroponic tower with local root-zone water retention, proprietary conductivity-based sensing, and a connected control interface. The mechanical design separates the shared reservoir from small water pockets at individual plant sites. These pockets buffer the interval between refill pulses, allowing the main pump to operate intermittently while plants retain access to water. The author describes a one-minute-per-hour refill schedule and approximately 10–24 hours of local reserve depending on plant uptake. This paper develops the corresponding water-balance and pump-duty models, relates the functional diagrams to the supplied CAD/video and assembled tower, and describes the measurement-to-interface responsibilities without publishing the sensor's construction or calibration recipe.

**Keywords:** hydroponic tower; local water reserve; intermittent pumping; modular CAD; conductivity sensing; connected control.

## Method
### 3.1 A local reserve changes the hydraulic requirement
Continuous pumping ties root access to continuous circulation. A retained pocket separates those requirements: a refill pulse replenishes water locally, then plant uptake and losses gradually reduce the reserve while the pump is off. Excess water returns through the shared circulation path; the pocket preserves a smaller volume at the plant site.

Let $V_p$ denote usable retained water, $q_{\mathrm{in}}$ refill flow, $q_u$ uptake, and $q_\ell$ combined evaporation and other losses. A lumped balance is

$$
\frac{\mathrm dV_p}{\mathrm dt}
=q_{\mathrm{in}}-q_u-q_\ell.
\tag{1}
$$

Between refill events, $q_{\mathrm{in}}=0$. Under an approximately constant demand, the reserve duration is

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{reserve}}\approx
\frac{V_{p,\mathrm{usable}}}{q_u+q_\ell}.
\tag{2}
$$

The author describes roughly 10–24 hours depending on plant absorption. This operating description is conditional: reserve volume, root access, plant size, environment, and losses affect duration. It is not a universal irrigation guarantee or a measured endurance curve. Root aeration and refill distribution remain relevant alongside water availability.

### 3.2 Intermittent pumping and the energy model
For one minute of pump operation in each hour, the duty ratio $D$ and daily pump runtime are

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&=\frac{1\ \mathrm{min}}{60\ \mathrm{min}}=\frac1{60}\approx1.67\%,\\
t_{\mathrm{on,day}}&=24\ \mathrm{min}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

At unchanged on-state pump power $P_{\mathrm{on}}$, an idealized daily energy model gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{pump}}&\approx P_{\mathrm{on}}D\,(24\ \mathrm h),\\
\frac{E_{\mathrm{pulse}}}{E_{\mathrm{continuous}}}&\approx\frac1{60}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

The analytical reduction applies to pump runtime and ideal pump energy. It excludes controller standby, lighting, startup losses, pump operating-point changes, and refill efficacy. No electrical power measurements are inferred from the CAD or screenshots.

### 3.3 Conductivity sensing as a calibrated proxy
The in-house sensing work uses an alternating measurement approach to obtain a conductivity-related response while avoiding a persistent DC bias. The research code includes repeated-cycle validation, temperature handling, calibration persistence, and separate invalid/saturated outcomes. Public explanation stops at these operating responsibilities; the excitation circuit, electrode geometry, timings, parts values, and fitted calibration parameters remain private.

Conductivity reflects the water's combined ionic composition. A general physical representation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa&=K_{\mathrm{cell}}G,\\
\kappa_{25}&\approx\frac{\kappa_T}{1+\alpha(T-25)},\\
C_{\mathrm{TDS}}&\approx f_{\mathrm{solution}}\kappa_{25}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

Here $G$ is conductance, $K_{\mathrm{cell}}$ a calibrated cell factor, $\alpha$ a temperature coefficient, and $f_{\mathrm{solution}}$ a composition-dependent conversion factor. These symbolic public relationships are not the proprietary production calibration equation. A TDS estimate cannot identify individual nutrient ions; temperature and composition matter to its interpretation. [USGS water-quality measurement background](https://water.usgs.gov/nwc/NWC/water_quality/tables/quality.abs.html).

## Implementation
### 4.1 Measurement, controller, and cloud responsibilities
The supplied Plantini firmware examples divide sensor processing from ESP32 control. Their handoff carries a reading together with status, quality, validity, and health information. The example controller requests readings and diagnostics over a serial interface, handles response timeouts, and distinguishes a valid measurement from an error response.

The author describes Firebase as the app/device synchronization layer. The architecture figure therefore separates sensor processing, controller state, shared cloud state, and the mobile interface. The supplied material establishes the sensing handoff and visible interface responsibilities; it does not establish the Flutter application's internal subscriptions or production database schema.

### 4.2 Configuration and observable state
The existing dashboard presents TDS, temperature, pH, pump state, and lighting state. The configuration screen provides a crop profile, a sensor-calibration action, and Always / Normal / Eco pump-mode choices. The captured numbers are interface states, not universal nutrient targets or plant-growth trial results.

The three mode labels demonstrate a control surface. Their exact production timing rules are not inferred from the labels. The one-minute-per-hour schedule discussed above is the author's hydraulic operating description. Likewise, a pH card does not by itself document a pH sensor implementation in the supplied TDS firmware.

## Discussion
Local buffering is the central mechanical idea: the device can replenish water periodically while maintaining a reserve at each root site. Its benefit depends on distribution across plant sites, usable pocket volume, plant demand, and losses. A useful validation record would pair refill uniformity, reserve depletion, root condition, and measured electrical energy over the same operating interval.

Connected sensing adds a second engineering requirement: the operator must understand whether a number is current, valid, calibrated, and associated with measured or assumed temperature. Bulk TDS trends support solution management, while nutrient-specific composition and crop outcomes require additional evidence. The paper presents the working design and source responsibilities without converting interface values or conceptual diagrams into performance claims.
